## Introduction
Sound is generated by vibrations in the air, which produce a continuous pressure waves.
Think of dropping a pebble in a pond -- the ripples that spread outward are similar to how sound waves
travel through the air.
In the recent article, we explored the basics of sound but only scratched the surface.

Now that we understand the basics of sound waves -- we can dive deeper into how we capture and work with them.

## From Air to Digital Data

Sound in the real world is continuous, but to store or process it digitally, we need to break it into smaller snapshots, a process called sampling. 

### Sampling, Nyquist Theorem & Aliasing

**Sampling** is a process of turning continuous waves into discrete data. A digital recording device takes "snapshots" of the analog waveform at regular time intervals. Each snapshot is called a *sample*.

From the [previous article]() we already know what **sample rate** is. Now let's talk about a crucial rule in digital 
audio - the **Nyquist-Shannon Sampling Theorem**.

**Aliasing** is a form of defect that occurs when a signal is sampled at a rate that is
too low to capture its highest frequencies accurately, causing higher-frequency components to "fold back" into lower frequencies. This results in distortion of the signal or unwanted artifacts, as the signal appears to contain
frequencies that weren't originally present. 

To prevent aliasing, the *sampling rate* must be **at least twice the highest frequency** in the signal, as stated by **Nyquist-Shannon Sampling Theorem**.

## Quantization 

After we broken down the auido waveform into discrete time intervals (*sampled the sound*), the next
step is **quantization**.

**Quantization** - the process of *rounding* the continuous amplitude values of the audio signal 
to the nearest discrete value, based on *bit depth (which we also explored in [previous article]())*.

*Well, but didn't we just sample the wave? Why are amplitudes still continuous then?*

You're absolutely right that the sound is sampled, and the waveform itself is no longer continuous 
in the **time** dimension. However, the **amplitude** of each sample is still considered continuous
because it can take an extremely large range of values.
To work with it in a digital system, we need to map these continuous amplitude values into a fixed number of
discrete levels. 

> Think of quantization like creating a fixed number of 'buckets', based on the bit depth.
>
> Each bucket represents a specific range of amplitude values. When we sample the audio, the amplitude
> of each sample is placed int othe bucket it fits best.
>
> The value of the sample is then replaced by the value of that bucket.
>
> This way, instead of having a infinite range of possible values, we now have a fixed set of amplitude levels.
> This is much easier to store and owrk with in digital systems.
